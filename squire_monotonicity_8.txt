# Riemann Hypothesis: From the "Narrow Steep Cone" to Rigorous Equivalents

> **Status:** Exploratory notes with numerical experiments, not a proof. Every rigorous criterion below is *equivalent* to the Riemann Hypothesis (RH), so the hard step is moved, not removed. Computations were done with mpmath and numpy and have not been independently audited. Literature statements come from sources found in November 2026; I read the abstracts and, for Lamzouri's paper, the full text, but did not check any proofs myself.

---

## Summary

**Bottom line.** The submitted "narrow steep cone" statement is not a valid argument: its key hypothesis assumes the conclusion. Rewriting it rigorously gives statements that are exactly equivalent to RH. Numerical checks are consistent with RH but cannot prove it, and the strongest recent theorems still leave room for a sparse set of off-line zeros.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Is the original claim a proof? | No. $E(\sigma)$ is never defined from $\zeta$, "endomorphism" is undefined, and $\operatorname{Re}\zeta(\rho)=0$ is trivial (Section 1). |
| What does the "cone" become? | $\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi>0$ for $\sigma>\tfrac12$, i.e. $\lvert\xi\rvert$ increases away from the line. The steepness is a pole at each zero ($\approx1/\delta$), not a linear cone, and the slope is exactly $0$ at $\sigma=\tfrac12$ between zeros (Section 4). |
| Rigorous equivalents tried | Li's criterion ($\lambda_n\ge0$ for all $n$, Section 3) and monotonicity of $\lvert\xi\rvert$ (Section 4). Both are equivalent to RH. |
| Do our numerics contradict RH? | No. $\lambda_n>0$ for $n\le150$ (exact); a direct $\zeta$ scan found no negative slope for $t\in[0.05,300]$ at five values of $\sigma$ and for $t\in[300,2000]$ at $\sigma=0.51$ (Sections 7, 11). |
| How strong is that evidence? | Weak. A planted off-line zero at height $\gamma$ would only show in Li's test at $n\sim\gamma^2$, and the monotonicity scan needs about $20\gamma$ evaluations of $\zeta$ (Section 8). Platt–Trudgian's verification up to $3\times10^{12}$ is far stronger. |
| What is proven today? | More than $67.25\%$ of zeros are simple and on the line (2026 preprints, Section 9); all zeros up to height $3\times10^{12}$ are on the line; zero-free regions only near $\sigma=1$. |
| Can that method reach RH? | No. Pair correlation is blind to sparse sets and to zeros within about $1/\log T$ of the line (Section 10). The certified fraction at $T=3\times10^5$ is $0.818$, converging slowly to $0.6725$. The finite inequality it rests on held in 80,000 random and 4,000 adversarial tests (Section 10.4). |

**Corrections made along the way.** An earlier draft of Section 10 used the Fejér kernel and attributed the constant $4/3$ to it; that was wrong, and the section now uses the Montgomery–Taylor kernel the theorem requires.

**Where things are.** Sections 1–6 give the claim and its rigorous replacements. Sections 7–8 are small-scale numerics and the planted-zero stress test. Section 9 surveys the literature. Sections 10–11 run the pair-correlation certificate, the Li test and the monotonicity scan at larger height. Section 12 lists open next steps.

## 1. Original Claim 

$$\left( \zeta \in \operatorname{End}(\Omega) \land \zeta(s) = \overline{\zeta(1 - \overline{s})} \land \forall \rho \in \mathcal{Z}(\zeta),\ \operatorname{Re}(\zeta(\rho)) = 0 \land E(\sigma) = \alpha \left| \sigma - \tfrac{1}{2} \right|,\ \alpha \gg 1 \right) \implies \forall \rho \in \mathcal{Z}(\zeta),\ \operatorname{Re}(\rho) = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

**Problems identified**

- $E(\sigma)$ is never defined in terms of $\zeta$, so the cone hypothesis effectively assumes the conclusion.
- "Endomorphism" is undefined: no structure is specified.
- The reflection condition is not the standard functional equation.
- $\operatorname{Re}\zeta(\rho)=0$ is trivially true at zeros, since $\zeta(\rho)=0$.
- "$\alpha\gg1$" is not a precise quantity.

## 2. Corrected Setup

The completed zeta function is entire:

$$\xi(s)=\tfrac12\, s(s-1)\,\pi^{-s/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{s}{2}\right)\zeta(s)$$

- Functional equation: $\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)$
- Real symmetry: $\xi(s)=\overline{\xi(\bar s)}$
- Zeros of $\xi$ are exactly the nontrivial zeros of $\zeta$, denoted $\mathcal{Z}(\xi)$, symmetric about the real axis and the line $\operatorname{Re}(s)=\tfrac12$.

## 3. Criterion A: Li's Criterion

**Li coefficients** (sum over nontrivial zeros in conjugate pairs):

$$\lambda_n=\sum_{\rho}\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{\rho}\right)^{n}\right]=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\,\frac{d^{n}}{ds^{n}}\Big[s^{\,n-1}\log\xi(s)\Big]_{s=1}$$

**Statement** (Li 1997; Bombieri–Lagarias 1999):

$$\Big(\forall n\ge 1:\ \lambda_n\ge 0\Big)\iff \forall\rho\in\mathcal{Z}(\xi):\ \operatorname{Re}(\rho)=\tfrac12$$

**Why it works.** Under $w=1-1/\rho$,

$$|w|=\frac{|\rho-1|}{|\rho|}=1\iff \operatorname{Re}(\rho)=\tfrac12$$

so the critical line maps to the unit circle.

- If RH holds, every $|w|=1$ and each $\lambda_n>0$.
- If a zero is off the line, its partner gives $|w|>1$. That term grows exponentially in $n$ and rotates in phase, forcing $\lambda_n<0$ for some $n$.

**Known results**

- $\lambda_1 = 1+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}-\tfrac12\log 4\pi \approx 0.0231>0$
- $\lambda_n>0$ verified numerically for a large range of $n$.
- Under RH: $\lambda_n \sim \tfrac{n}{2}\left(\log n - 1 + \gamma - \log 2\pi\right)$.
- Proving $\lambda_n\ge0$ for all $n$ unconditionally is equivalent to RH.

## 4. Criterion B: Monotonicity of $|\xi|$ (squire rigorous "cone")

Fix a height $t$ and set $E_t(\sigma)=\log|\xi(\sigma+it)|$.

### 4.1 What the cone idea becomes

- By $\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)$ and real symmetry, $E_t(\sigma)$ is **even** about $\sigma=\tfrac12$. A cone with vertex at $\tfrac12$ is therefore the natural picture.
- Between zeros, $E_t$ is **smooth** at $\tfrac12$ with slope exactly $0$. So the subdifferential form $[-\alpha,\alpha]\subseteq\partial E(\tfrac12)$ is **false** there. Numerically, the slope at $\sigma=\tfrac12$ is about $10^{-22}$ for $t=5,10,20,30$.
- The steepness appears only **at zeros**, and it is a pole, not a linear cone.

### 4.2 Slope as a sum over zeros

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\log|\xi(\sigma+it)|=\operatorname{Re}\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(s)=\sum_{\rho}\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{s-\rho}$$

A zero $\rho=\tfrac12+i\gamma$ on the line contributes

$$\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{s-\rho}=\frac{\sigma-\tfrac12}{|s-\rho|^2}$$

which is $\approx 1/\delta$ at distance $\delta=\sigma-\tfrac12$ when $t=\gamma$. The "$\alpha\gg1$" of the original cone is really a logarithmic spike of unbounded steepness at each zero.

**Numerical check near the first zero** ($\gamma\approx14.1347$, $t=\gamma$):

| $\delta$ | $\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi$ | $1/\delta$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 10.0068 | 10 |
| 0.05 | 20.0034 | 20 |
| 0.01 | 100.0007 | 100 |

### 4.3 The criterion

$$\textbf{RH}\iff \operatorname{Re}\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(\sigma+it)>0\quad\text{for all }\sigma>\tfrac12,\ t\in\mathbb{R}$$

Equivalently, $|\xi(\sigma+it)|$ is strictly increasing in $\sigma$ for $\sigma>\tfrac12$ at every fixed $t$.

- **(⇒)** Under RH every zero contributes a positive term for $\sigma>\tfrac12$.
- **(⇐)** A zero off the line at $\beta>\tfrac12$ puts a pole of $\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{s-\rho}$ there, so the slope goes negative on one side of it.

**Numerical scan** ($t\in[0.1,60]$, 1200 points), minimum of $\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi$:

| $\sigma$ | Minimum | Attained at |
|---|---|---|
| 0.51 | 0.0005 | $t=0.1$ |
| 0.55 | 0.0023 | $t=0.1$ |
| 0.60 | 0.0046 | $t=0.1$ |
| 0.75 | 0.0116 | $t=0.1$ |

The slope stays positive throughout, consistent with RH on this range. This is not a proof, since the criterion must hold for all $t$.

### 4.4 What does not help

- **Subharmonicity:** $\log|\xi|$ is subharmonic everywhere, with or without off-line zeros, so it carries no location information.
- **Pointwise convexity in $\sigma$:** $\partial_\sigma^2\log|\xi|=-\partial_t^2\log|\xi|$ has no fixed sign.

## 5. Mapping the Original Idea to Rigorous Statements

| Original idea | Rigorous counterpart |
|---|---|
| "Endomorphism" structure | $\xi$ entire |
| Reflection axis $\sigma=\tfrac12$ | $\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)$ |
| $\operatorname{Re}\zeta(\rho)=0$ | Dropped (trivial) |
| Narrow steep cone, $\alpha\gg1$ | $\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi>0$ for $\sigma>\tfrac12$ (unbounded steepness near zeros) |
| Subdifferential $[-\alpha,\alpha]$ | False between zeros; slope is $0$ at $\sigma=\tfrac12$ |
| Implication to $\operatorname{Re}\rho=\tfrac12$ | Equivalent to RH, not a derivation of it |

## 6. Other Equivalent Forms

- Robin's inequality for the divisor function.
- de Bruijn–Newman constant: $\Lambda\ge0$ is proven (Rodgers–Tao); $\Lambda\le0$ is equivalent to RH.

## 7. Small-Scale Numerical Checks

![Numerics](rh_numerics.png)

**Monotonicity scan.** $t\in[0.05,300]$ in steps of $0.05$, at $\sigma\in\{0.501,0.51,0.55,0.6,0.75\}$. $\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi$ was positive at every point (0 non-positive values). The minimum of $\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi\,/\,(\sigma-\tfrac12)$ is about $0.046$, attained as $t\to0$, and is the same for every $\sigma$ tested. Spikes of size $\sim 1/\delta$ occur at the zeros.

**Li coefficients.** Computed from the Taylor series of $\log\xi$ at $s=1$ (via a 512-point contour on $|s-1|=10$, 80-digit arithmetic), using $\lambda_n=n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}c_k$ where $\log\xi(1+u)=\sum c_k u^k$.

| $n$ | $\lambda_n$ | RH asymptotic |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.02310 | −1.130 |
| 2 | 0.09235 | −1.568 |
| 5 | 0.5755 | −1.628 |
| 10 | 2.2793 | 0.210 |
| 20 | 8.7693 | 7.351 |
| 50 | 43.531 | 41.284 |
| 100 | 118.60 | 117.23 |
| 150 | 207.68 | 206.25 |

All $\lambda_n$ for $1\le n\le150$ are positive, $\lambda_1$ matches the closed form, and the gap to the asymptotic shrinks in relative terms. This is consistent with RH but proves nothing, since the criterion needs all $n$.

## 8. Stress Test: Planting a Fake Off-Line Zero

To see what the two criteria can actually detect, I added a hypothetical zero quadruple $\beta\pm i\gamma,\ 1-\beta\pm i\gamma$ (as required by the symmetries) to the real zero set and asked when each test would notice.

![Planted zero](rh_planted_zero.png)

**Li test.** The planted zero $1-\beta+i\gamma$ maps to $w$ with $|w|>1$, and its contribution grows like $|w|^n$. The coefficient $\lambda_n$ first goes negative when this growth overtakes the $\tfrac n2\log n$ baseline. Baseline: exact $\lambda_n$ for $n\le150$, the RH asymptotic beyond. The two removed on-line pairs at $\gamma\pm1$ are subtracted.

| $\gamma$ | $\beta=0.55$ | $\beta=0.6$ | $\beta=0.75$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14.2 | $4.65\times10^{4}$ | $2.15\times10^{4}$ | $7.7\times10^{3}$ |
| 100 | $3.2\times10^{6}$ | $1.5\times10^{6}$ | $5.7\times10^{5}$ |
| 1,000 | $4.3\times10^{8}$ | $2.1\times10^{8}$ | $7.8\times10^{7}$ |
| 10,000 | $5.3\times10^{10}$ | $2.6\times10^{10}$ | $9.9\times10^{9}$ |
| 100,000 | $\approx6\times10^{12}$ | $\approx3\times10^{12}$ | $\approx1.2\times10^{12}$ |

(First negative $n$; the last row is the amplitude-crossing estimate $n^*$, not brute-forced. Runs were checked by brute-force scan for $\gamma\le10^4$.)

The required $n$ scales roughly like $\gamma^2/(2\beta-1)$ times a logarithm. Even a zero as low as $\gamma=14.2$ off the line by only $0.05$ would not be seen until $n\approx4.6\times10^4$, far beyond the $n\le150$ checked above.

**Monotonicity test.** With the quadruple at $\gamma=100$, $\beta=0.6$, the slope $\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi$ at $\sigma=0.55$ goes negative only for $t\in[99.916,\,100.084]$, a window about $0.17$ wide. At $\beta=0.75$, $\sigma=0.6$ the window is about $0.41$ wide. So the scan needs a step of roughly $0.05$ or finer and must reach $t\approx\gamma$, i.e. about $20\gamma$ evaluations of $\zeta$.

**What this shows**

- Both tests do detect a planted zero, so they are not vacuous.
- The monotonicity test is much cheaper per height: it needs only a scan up to $\gamma$, whereas Li's test needs $n\sim\gamma^2$.
- The small-scale checks ($t\le300$, $n\le150$) rule out an off-line zero with $\gamma\lesssim300$ via the scan and say nothing about higher zeros (Section 11 extends the scan to $t\le2000$). The known verification that the first $\sim10^{13}$ zeros lie on the line is far stronger than anything run here.
- Finite checks can never prove RH. They can only push the lowest possible counterexample higher.

## 9. State of the Evidence (searched November 2026)

### 9.1 Proportion of zeros proven to lie on the critical line

| Year | Result | Proportion on the line |
|---|---|---|
| 1914 | Hardy | Infinitely many |
| 1942 | Selberg | A positive proportion |
| 1974 | Levinson | > 1/3 |
| 1989 | Conrey | > 2/5 (about 40.8%) |
| 2011-12 | Bui–Conrey–Young; Feng | > 41%; > 41.28% |
| 2019-20 | Pratt–Robles–Zaharescu–Zeindler | > 5/12 (about 41.7%) |
| **Aug 2026** | **Alpöge–Furman (argument produced by an internal Claude model)** | **> 2/3 are simple *and* on the line; later optimized to > 67.25%** |

- The 2026 result is on arXiv (2608.13637), with a companion Lean 4 formalization released by Anthropic. The Breakthrough Tracker lists it as a Lean-verified preprint, **not yet peer reviewed** (as of its August 2026 entry).
- Lamzouri (arXiv 2609.02882, September 2026) gives an independent, shorter proof of the same bounds (> 67.25% simple and on the line, > 83.62% distinct). His appendix cites Lean certificates for part of his argument from Axiom Math's AxiomProver.
- The method uses pair correlation (Montgomery–Taylor constant $C_0=\tfrac32-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\cot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\approx0.6725$) and an explicit-formula second moment. It is insensitive to $o(N)$ off-line zeros, so it **cannot** rule out a sparse set of exceptions.
- It does **not** prove RH. About 32.7% of zeros are still not shown to be on the line.

### 9.2 Computation

- Platt–Trudgian (2021): all $12{,}363{,}153{,}437{,}138$ zeros with height up to $3{,}000{,}175{,}332{,}800$ lie on the critical line and are simple.
- This is the range in which Section 8 shows only a violation at huge height or huge $n$ could hide. Nothing in our small-scale checks competes with it.

### 9.3 Zero-free regions and zero density

- **Classical region:** no zeros for $\sigma \ge 1-\dfrac{1}{5.558691\log|t|}$, $|t|\ge2$ (Mossinghoff–Trudgian–Yang, 2024).
- **Vinogradov–Korobov region:** no zeros for $\sigma\ge1-\dfrac{c}{(\log t)^{2/3}(\log\log t)^{1/3}}$; Ford's explicit constant is about 57.54.
- **Zero density:** a recent exponent $30/13$ in $N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{b(1-\sigma)}$ appears in the literature I found (attributed to Guth–Maynard, 2024, from memory; the source I read did not name the authors). Such estimates bound how many zeros can sit away from the line, not whether any do.
- Zero-free regions only exclude zeros near $\sigma=1$ (and, by symmetry, near $\sigma=0$). Zero-density estimates thin out the rest, but nothing excludes zeros between those regions and the critical line.

### 9.4 How this fits our two criteria

- **Li's criterion** is global: it needs $\lambda_n\ge0$ for all $n$. The proportion results give no direct handle on $\lambda_n$.
- **Monotonicity** ($\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi>0$ for $\sigma>\tfrac12$) holds for $\sigma\ge1$, since every zero has $\beta<1$ and so each term $\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{s-\rho}$ is positive there. The open part is $\tfrac12<\sigma<1$, where the zero-free regions leave room.
- A positive-proportion theorem and a "no zeros off the line" theorem are different in kind. The first can be approached by averaging (moments, pair correlation); RH needs control of every single zero.

## 10. Testing the Pair-Correlation Certificate

The 2026 results rest on an inequality (Lamzouri, Prop. 2.1). For a finite multiset $\mathcal Z$ invariant under complex conjugation, and $K=\widehat{\eta^{2}}$ with $\eta$ even and compactly supported,

$$\#\{\text{simple real elements}\}\ \ge\ 2|\mathcal Z|-\sum_{z,s\in\mathcal Z}K(z-s)^2 .$$

For zeta, $z_\rho=i(\rho-\tfrac12)\tfrac{\log T}{2\pi}$ is real exactly when $\operatorname{Re}\rho=\tfrac12$. The theorem needs $f=\eta^2$ supported in $[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$, so that $f*f$ lies in $[-1,1]$, where the unconditional pair-correlation formula applies. I use the Montgomery–Taylor kernel

$$f_0(x)=\frac{\cos(\sqrt2\,x)}{\sqrt2\,\sin(1/\sqrt2)}\ \ (|x|\le\tfrac12),\qquad K=\widehat{f_0},$$

whose asymptotic constant is $C_{\rm MT}=\tfrac12+\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\cot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\approx1.3275$, giving a certified fraction $2-C_{\rm MT}\approx0.6725$. (Lamzouri smooths $f_0$ near the endpoints; I use it unsmoothed and truncate pair sums at $|z-s|\le200$, which changes the sums by roughly $10^{-3}$ relatively.)

> **Correction.** An earlier draft of this section used the Fejér kernel $K=\operatorname{sinc}^2$ and described it as giving Montgomery's constant $4/3$. That was wrong: that $K$ comes from a $\lambda=1$ window outside the range of the unconditional formula, and the quantity summed is $K^2$, not $K$. The inequality itself holds for any such kernel, so the sanity check was still valid, but the numbers below replace the earlier ones.

### 10.1 Planted off-line zeros (first 1000 zeros, $T\approx1419$)

Two adjacent zeros are replaced by $\tfrac12\pm d+i\tilde\gamma$ at their midpoint, so $N$ is unchanged.

![Pair correlation](rh_pair_correlation.png)

| Moved off line | $d=0.001$ | $d=0.01$ | $d=0.1$ | $d=0.3$ | True simple-on-line |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% (baseline) | 0.901 | 0.901 | 0.901 | 0.901 | 1.000 |
| 1% | 0.892 | 0.891 | 0.890 | 0.866 | 0.990 |
| 5% | 0.854 | 0.855 | 0.849 | 0.721 | 0.950 |
| 10% | 0.810 | 0.810 | 0.800 | 0.543 | 0.900 |
| 20% | 0.722 | 0.717 | 0.683 | 0.179 | 0.800 |
| 30% | 0.627 | 0.629 | 0.579 | −0.186 | 0.700 |

(Entries are the certified lower bound divided by $N$.)

1. **The inequality held in all 24 configurations** (certified bound ≤ true count).
2. **Near-line off-line zeros look like double zeros.** The symmetry puts $z$ and $\bar z$ at the same real position, separated vertically by $2d\log T/2\pi$. For $d\lesssim0.01$ that is about $0.02$, far below the kernel's resolution. The bound drops one-for-one with the true count, and $d=0.001$ is indistinguishable from $d=0.01$.
3. **Far-off zeros are penalized.** At $d=0.3$, moving 30% of zeros pushes the bound below zero, and the penalty grows quickly with $d$. This is the mechanism behind the horizontal-distribution results (Baluyot–Goldston–Suriajaya–Turnage-Butterbaugh): most zeros must lie in a box of width about $1/\log T$ around the line, though not necessarily on it.

### 10.2 The real zeros at larger height

Zeros were found by sign changes of the Riemann–Siegel $Z$-function (first 1000 from mpmath). The method reproduced the first 971 zeros above $t=100$ with maximum error $0.002$, and the count up to $3\times10^5$ is $466{,}608$ against $466{,}659$ from the smoothed counting formula. The sum $\sum K(z-s)^2$ runs over all zeros with $0<\gamma\le T$, $z=\gamma\log T/2\pi$.

| $T$ | $N$ | $\log T$ | $\sum K^2/N$ | Certified fraction $2-\sum K^2/N$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10^3$ | 649 | 6.91 | 1.0892 | 0.911 |
| $3\times10^3$ | 2,469 | 8.01 | 1.1132 | 0.887 |
| $10^4$ | 10,142 | 9.21 | 1.1352 | 0.865 |
| $3\times10^4$ | 35,673 | 10.31 | 1.1532 | 0.847 |
| $10^5$ | 138,059 | 11.51 | 1.1695 | 0.831 |

**Reading the table**

- The certified fraction falls steadily toward the theoretical $0.6725$, but only like $1/\log T$. At $T=3\times10^5$ it is still $0.818$.
- Fitting $a+b/\log T$ and $a+b/\log T+c/\log^2T$ to these six points gives limits of $\sum K^2/N\approx1.293$ and $1.351$, which bracket $C_{\rm MT}=1.3275$. This agrees with theory, though it is only an extrapolation.
- All $466{,}608$ zeros here are on the line, so these are baseline values for the true zeta. The bound is a theorem about the real zeros, not a measurement of them.

### 10.3 Direct check of the finite inequality (Prop. 2.1)

The proposition is a statement about any finite multiset $\mathcal Z$ invariant under complex conjugation, so it can be tested on arbitrary sets, not just zeta zeros. With $N=|\mathcal Z|$ (multiplicities counted), $\Sigma=\sum_{z,s}K(z-s)^2$ and $A=\Sigma/N$, I checked four of its inequalities:

| Eq. | Statement |
|---|---|
| (2.4) | #simple real elements $\ \ge\ 2N-\Sigma$ |
| (2.5) | #distinct elements $\ \ge\ \tfrac32N-\tfrac12\Sigma$ |
| (2.6) | #simple elements + #real elements $\ \ge\ 3N-\Sigma$ |
| (2.7) | if $1\le A<2$: #(real or simple) $\ \ge\ \dfrac{5+2\sqrt2-2A}{3+2\sqrt2}\,N$ |

**Kernels** (each $K=\widehat f$ with $f=\eta^2\ge0$, $\int f=1$): Montgomery–Taylor, Fejér (triangle), indicator, and raised cosine $1+\cos2\pi x$. The inequality only needs $\eta\in L^2$ with compact support, so the support range needed for the asymptotic formula is irrelevant here.

**Random sets.** 20,000 per kernel, 80,000 in total: 1 to 12 distinct points, real or conjugate pairs with imaginary part from $10^{-6}$ to $2.5$, multiplicities 1 to 3, and spacings from clustered to far apart. **Violations: 0.** ((2.7) applied to between 783 and 998 sets per kernel.)

**Adversarial search.** 250 Nelder–Mead runs per kernel per inequality (4,000 in total) minimizing the normalized slack (left side minus right side, divided by $N$) over positions, real/complex structure and multiplicities. The lowest slack found was about $-4\times10^{-16}$, which is floating-point rounding. The optimizer reaches equality but never crosses it.

**Tight cases** (Montgomery–Taylor kernel):

| Set | Slack in (2.4) |
|---|---|
| One isolated simple real point | $0$ |
| 10 far-apart simple real points | $5\times10^{-10}$ |
| 5 far-apart *double* real points | $9\times10^{-10}$ |
| Conjugate pair $x\pm 0.001i$ (no real elements) | $2.4\times10^{-5}$ |
| Two simple reals $0.5$ apart | $0.87$ |

So (2.4) is sharp in the isolated simple case and in the isolated double case ($2-m$ with $m=2$), and the near-real conjugate pair is almost tight, matching the earlier observation that a zero just off the line looks like a double zero.

**Negative controls** (to confirm the harness can fail):

- Dropping conjugation invariance, $\mathcal Z=\{0.2+0.4i\}$: (2.4) is violated (needs $0\ge1.00$).
- A too-strong version with $\tfrac12\Sigma$ in place of $\Sigma$: violated in 2,917 of 3,000 random real sets.
- A kernel with $K(0)=0.8$ instead of $1$: (2.4) violated in 2,600 of 3,000.

**What this does and does not show.** It confirms that my implementation of the kernel and the sums matches the proposition on a wide range of inputs, including the edge cases, and that the hypotheses ($K(0)=1$, conjugation invariance) matter. It does not check the asymptotic formulas (Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2), which are the analytic heart of the argument; Section 10.2 only compares them against real zeros numerically. Equation (2.7) follows from my reading of the printed statement, in which "real or simple" counts multiplicity.

### 10.4 Conclusion

The pair-correlation method certifies a proportion by showing that off-line or multiple zeros cannot be too numerous, while staying blind to any single zero or any sparse set. That is why it cannot approach RH, which requires every zero to be on the line.

## 11. Larger-Height Zeros Through the Li and Monotonicity Tests

The zero set from Section 10.2 (all 466,608 zeros up to $T=3\times10^5$) was run through both earlier tests.

### 11.1 Li coefficients from zeros

With all zeros on the line, $\rho=\tfrac12\pm i\gamma$ and $w=1-1/\rho$ has $|w|=1$, so

$$\lambda_n=\sum_{\gamma>0}2\bigl(1-\cos n\varphi_\gamma\bigr),\qquad \varphi_\gamma=2\arctan\tfrac1{2\gamma}.$$

I summed over zeros up to $T$ and added the part beyond $T$ by integrating against the density $\tfrac1{2\pi}\log\tfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}$. Comparison with the exact $\lambda_n$ from the Taylor-series method (Section 7):

| $T$ | Max relative error, $n\le150$, zeros only | With tail |
|---|---|---|
| $10^3$ | $1.0\times10^{-1}$ | $4.2\times10^{-5}$ |
| $10^4$ | $1.4\times10^{-2}$ | $1.6\times10^{-6}$ |
| $10^5$ | $1.8\times10^{-3}$ | $1.3\times10^{-6}$ |
| $3\times10^5$ | $6.8\times10^{-4}$ | $1.3\times10^{-6}$ |

Example, $n=100$: exact $118.6038$; zeros up to $3\times10^5$ give $118.5413$, and $118.6037$ with the tail.

- The two independent computations of $\lambda_n$ agree to about $10^{-6}$ relative accuracy, which checks both the zero data and the Taylor-series values.
- **Positivity is automatic here.** Each on-line zero contributes $2(1-\cos n\varphi)\ge0$, so truncated sums are positive by construction (checked for sampled $n\le3000$). The test can only detect a violation through a zero off the line, which for $\gamma$ at this height would need $n\sim\gamma^2$ (Section 8).
- For $n\gtrsim1000$ the numerical tail integral gave convergence warnings; those values (e.g. $n=1000$: $2326$ vs asymptotic $2323.6$) are approximate.

### 11.2 Monotonicity of $|\xi|$

**Zero-sum formula.** For on-line zeros, $\operatorname{Re}\,\xi'/\xi(\sigma+it)=\sum_\gamma\dfrac{\delta}{\delta^2+(t-\gamma)^2}$ with $\delta=\sigma-\tfrac12$. It agrees with a direct evaluation of $\zeta$ at six sample points to about $10^{-3}$ (e.g. $\sigma=0.55$, $t=2000$: direct $0.617131$, zeros $0.616954$).

**Why a zero-based scan proves nothing.** Every term is positive, so the sum is positive by construction. The only real test is a direct evaluation of $\zeta$, whose slope would turn negative near an off-line zero.

**Direct scan.** $\sigma=0.51$, $t\in[300,2000]$, step $0.1$ (17,000 points; 1,394 zeros in this window), using $\zeta'/\zeta$ and $\Gamma'/\Gamma$ directly:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Points with slope $\le0$ | **0** |
| Minimum slope | $0.0163$ at $t=308.6$ (ratio to $\delta$: $1.63$) |
| Median slope | $0.136$ |
| Maximum slope | $100.1$ (pole value $1/\delta=100$) |

**Direct vs. on-line prediction.** Largest deviation $5.36$ at $t=1573.8$; rms $0.16$. The deviation depends on distance to the nearest zero:

| Distance to nearest zero | Points | Max deviation |
|---|---|---|
| $<0.02$ | 539 | 5.36 |
| $0.02$–$0.05$ | 840 | 1.83 |
| $0.05$–$0.1$ | 1,378 | 0.165 |
| $0.1$–$0.2$ | 2,737 | 0.023 |
| $\ge0.2$ | 11,506 | 0.0037 |

The large values sit within $0.05$ of a zero, where the pole width ($\delta=0.01$) is comparable to the $\sim0.002$ error in my Riemann–Siegel zero positions. Away from zeros the prediction holds to $4\times10^{-3}$. A planted off-line zero at $\beta=0.6$ would add a negative pole of size about $1/(0.6-\sigma)\approx11$ spread over a window of width several tenths, far larger than these deviations (Section 8), so none is present in $[300,2000]$ at this resolution.

**Limits.** The scan covers $t\le2000$, a tiny part of the range already verified by Platt–Trudgian. The evaluation of $\zeta$ at much larger heights was too slow here.
